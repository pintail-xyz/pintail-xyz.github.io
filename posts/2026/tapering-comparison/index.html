Offset vs. Blend-and-Offset: Comparing Two Linear Tapering Approaches

Both proposals apply a linear offset to bring Ethereum consensus-layer issuance to zero at 50% staked. The difference is whether the base reward formula itself is also modified. Despite sharing the same calibration point and the same zero at saturation, the yield curves take genuinely different shapes.
Published

May 17, 2026

Two approaches to linear issuance tapering have been explored in earlier posts. The simpler one leaves get_base_reward_per_increment — the \(1/\sqrt{f}\) formula — untouched and adds a separate linear offset that cancels net issuance at 50% staked. The more structured one first modifies the base reward formula to blend linearly from the \(1/\sqrt{f}\) curve at low staking to a flat level at saturation, and then applies a matching linear offset on top. In both cases, calibrating BASE_REWARD_FACTOR to reproduce today’s consensus-layer yield at \(\sim\!33\%\) staked makes them directly comparable.

The question this post answers: with matched calibration, do the net yield curves actually differ?

The two formulas

Let \(r_{64}(f) = B_0 E / \sqrt{f \cdot S \cdot 10^9}\) be the current consensus-layer yield at staking ratio \(f\) (\(B_0 = 64\)), and write \(u = f/f_\text{sat}\) for the normalised staking level (\(f_\text{sat} = 50\%\), \(r_\text{sat} = r_{64}(f_\text{sat}) \approx 2.14\%\)).

Approach A — offset only

The base reward formula is unchanged; BASE_REWARD_FACTOR is raised from 64 to \(K_A \times 64\). A new function get_base_reward_offset_per_increment returns a per-epoch deduction that grows linearly with total staking from zero to the gross base reward at saturation:

\[\text{offset}(f) = K_A \cdot u \cdot r_\text{sat}\]

The net yield is:

\[r_A(f) = K_A \cdot \max\!\bigl(0,\; r_{64}(f) - u\,r_\text{sat}\bigr)\]

Using \(r_\text{sat}/r_{64}(f) = u^{1/2}\) (since \(r_{64}(f) \propto f^{-1/2}\)), this simplifies to:

\[\boxed{r_A(f) = K_A\,r_{64}(f)\cdot\max\!\bigl(0,\; 1 - u^{3/2}\bigr)}\]

The factor \(u^{3/2}\) appears because \(r_{64}(f) \propto u^{-1/2}\) — the offset is proportional to \(u\) but the gross yield is proportional to \(u^{-1/2}\), so their ratio is \(u^{3/2}\).

Approach B — linear blend of base reward, then offset

get_base_reward_per_increment is modified to blend linearly between the \(K_B\)-scaled current formula and the flat level \(r_\text{sat}\) (the value the original \(B_0 = 64\) formula gives at saturation):

\[\tilde{r}(f) = (1-u)\,K_B\,r_{64}(f) + u\,r_\text{sat}, \qquad f \le f_\text{sat}; \quad r_\text{sat} \text{ above}\]

A separate offset function then adds the same linear deduction:

\[\text{offset}(f) = u \cdot r_\text{sat}\]

Subtracting, the \(r_\text{sat}\) terms cancel exactly:

\[\tilde{r}(f) - \text{offset}(f) = (1-u)\,K_B\,r_{64}(f) + \cancel{u\,r_\text{sat}} - \cancel{u\,r_\text{sat}}\]

\[\boxed{r_B(f) = K_B\,(1-u)\,r_{64}(f)}\]

The blend was needed to construct the gross yield and to motivate the offset shape, but the net formula is simply the existing \(r_{64}\) scaled down by the linear weight \((1-u)\).

Calibration

Both proposals are calibrated so the net yield at the current staking ratio \(f_0 \approx \tfrac{1}{3}\) (\(u_0 = \tfrac{2}{3}\)) equals today’s consensus-layer yield \(r_{64}(f_0)\).

Approach A: \(K_A \cdot r_{64}(f_0) \cdot (1 - u_0^{3/2}) = r_{64}(f_0)\), giving:

\[K_A = \frac{1}{1 - u_0^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{1-(2/3)^{3/2}} \approx 2.195 \implies B_A \approx 140\]

Approach B: \(K_B \cdot (1 - u_0) \cdot r_{64}(f_0) = r_{64}(f_0)\), giving:

\[K_B = \frac{1}{1-u_0} = \frac{1}{1 - 2/3} = 3 \implies B_B = 192\]

Both \(B_A \approx 140\) and \(B_B = 192\) are close multiples of \(B_0 = 64\), but only \(B_B\) is an exact integer multiple (\(3 \times 64\)).

Do the yield curves differ?

Yes. The net taper functions — the multipliers applied to \(r_{64}(f)\) — are:

\[\text{A:}\quad K_A\bigl(1 - u^{3/2}\bigr) \qquad \text{B:}\quad K_B\,(1-u)\]

Both vanish at \(u = 1\) and both equal 1 at \(u_0 = 2/3\) by construction. But \(u^{3/2}\) and \(u\) are different functions of \(u\), so the curves disagree at every other staking ratio.

The crossover at the calibration point is not a coincidence — it is exactly what calibration means. The two curves diverge away from that point in opposite directions:

Staking ratio Current Offset-only (A) Blend+offset (B) B vs A
10% 4.8% 9.6% 11.5% B higher by 20%
25% 3.0% 4.3% 4.5% B higher by 6%
33% 2.6% 2.6% 2.6% equal (calibration)
40% 2.4% 1.5% 1.4% A higher by 4%
45% 2.3% 0.72% 0.68% A higher by 6%

The differences are largest at low staking. Above the calibration point the two curves are very close, with A retaining a small edge.

Why does A remain slightly more generous above 33%? The offset in A is proportional to \(u\) (linear in staking), but the gross reward decays as \(u^{-1/2}\). Near saturation, the gross decays slower than the offset grows, leaving more net reward per unit than the linear \((1-u)\) decay of Approach B. Mathematically, \(1 - u^{3/2} > (K_B/K_A)(1-u)\) for \(u > u_0\) because the calibration ratio \(K_B/K_A = 3/2.195 \approx 1.37\) is not large enough to compensate.

Total issuance

The issuance peak \(f^*\) determines the boundary of the aggregate anti-discouragement property. For \(f < f^*\), total net issuance grows with staking — a discouragement attack shrinks the reward pool — so the collective deterrent is intact. For \(f > f^*\), the property breaks down.

The difference in peak location follows from the two taper functions. Annual issuance is \(I(f) = r(f) \cdot f \cdot S\), and with \(r_{64}(f) \propto u^{-1/2}\) this is proportional to \(t(u) \cdot u^{1/2}\) where \(t(u)\) is the taper function. Differentiating each and setting to zero:

\[\frac{d}{du}\bigl[(1 - u^{3/2})\,u^{1/2}\bigr] = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}} - 2u = 0 \implies u^{3/2} = \tfrac{1}{4} \implies u = 2^{-4/3}, \quad f^*_A = f_\text{sat}\cdot 2^{-4/3} = 2^{-7/3} \approx 19.8\%\]

\[\frac{d}{du}\bigl[(1-u)\,u^{1/2}\bigr] = \frac{1-3u}{2\sqrt{u}} = 0 \implies u = \tfrac{1}{3}, \quad f^*_B = f_\text{sat}/3 \approx 16.7\%\]

Both lie well below the current \(\sim\!33\%\) staking ratio. The anti-discouragement property therefore does not hold at current staking for either proposal — excess staking is already in the region where total issuance falls as staking grows, providing continuous automatic pressure against further staking growth.

Slope at saturation

Both proposals arrive at zero with a kink — a non-zero slope. Computing at \(f = f_\text{sat}\) (\(u = 1\)):

\[\left.\frac{dr_A}{df}\right|_{f_\text{sat}} = -\frac{3K_A\,r_\text{sat}}{2f_\text{sat}} \approx -6.58\,r_\text{sat} \approx -14.1\% \text{ per percentage point of staking}\]

\[\left.\frac{dr_B}{df}\right|_{f_\text{sat}} = -\frac{K_B\,r_\text{sat}}{f_\text{sat}} = -6\,r_\text{sat} \approx -12.9\% \text{ per percentage point of staking}\]

Approach B arrives about 9% more gently than Approach A. The difference traces back to the blend: the modified gross yield in B is already softening toward \(r_\text{sat}\) as \(f \to f_\text{sat}\), which partially offsets the steepening due to the offset. In Approach A, the gross reward continues on its \(1/\sqrt{f}\) trajectory right up to the saturation point, so the offset must do all the work of bringing the net to zero.

Penalty implications

In both proposals, the offset is applied only to rewards, not to penalties. Inactivity penalties and slashing are proportional to get_base_reward_per_increment, which is the gross base reward — not the offset-reduced net.

Approach A: the gross base reward is \(K_A \cdot r_{64}(f)\) at all staking ratios, so penalties are a constant \(K_A \approx 2.20\times\) current levels regardless of how much ETH is staked.

Approach B: the gross base reward is the blended value \(\tilde{r}(f) = (1-u)\,K_B\,r_{64}(f) + u\,r_\text{sat}\). Dividing by the current penalty \(r_{64}(f)\) gives a multiplier of:

\[\text{B penalty multiplier} = K_B(1-u) + u^{3/2}\]

(using \(r_\text{sat}/r_{64}(f) = u^{1/2}\)). This starts at \(K_B = 3\times\) at zero staking and declines to exactly \(1\times\) at saturation, where the blended gross equals \(r_\text{sat} = r_{64}(f_\text{sat})\) — the value the current formula gives at 50% staked.

50% \(\approx 2.20\times\) \(= 1.00\times\)

Below \(\sim\!17\%\) staking, B imposes higher penalties than A. Above \(\sim\!17\%\) — which includes the current staking ratio and any plausible future level — A imposes higher penalties.

Approach A’s penalty is simple and constant: a fixed \(2.2\times\) uplift that is predictable for any validator at any staking level. Approach B’s penalty varies with staking, declining through current levels at \(\sim\!33\%\) and fading back to today’s unmodified penalty rate at saturation. Whether a penalty structure that weakens as staking grows toward the cap is desirable is an open design question.

Spec architecture

Both proposals require BASE_REWARD_FACTOR to change and both require a new offset function. The single difference is what happens to get_base_reward_per_increment:

  • Approach A: the function is unchanged in form; only the constant \(B\) increases. The same 1/√f formula continues to operate, just calibrated to a higher level.
  • Approach B: the function is rewritten to implement the linear blend — it must now look up the saturation balance, compute the deficit, and evaluate the two-term interpolation before returning the blended base reward.

The modified get_base_reward_per_increment in Approach B is more complex to audit and verify, since it introduces a conditional structure that Approach A avoids.

Summary

The two proposals are more similar than they might appear: both use a linear offset, both are calibrated to preserve current yields at 33% staking, and both drive net issuance to zero at 50%. But they are not equivalent.

Approach A (offset only) is the more transparent design. The \(1/\sqrt{f}\) formula is untouched; any reader of the spec can see immediately that the base reward mechanism has not changed. The penalty structure is simple and stable: validators always face a constant multiple of today’s penalties. The trade-off is that the offset alone — without any modification of the base reward curve — produces a net yield that arrives at zero with a slightly steeper slope, and retains a slightly higher yield above 33% than Approach B.

Approach B (blend + offset) modifies the base reward curve to approach a flat level at saturation before the offset brings it to zero. This produces a gentler arrival at zero issuance and a more responsive stabiliser at very low staking (higher yields below 33%). The cost is a more complex get_base_reward_per_increment, and a penalty structure that varies with staking: higher than A at low staking, lower than A above \(\sim\!17\%\), and declining to \(1\times\) the current rate at saturation — the same level the unmodified formula would give at 50% staked.
