Smooth Yield Pinch-off: The Multiplicative Approach

Why polynomial staking offsets — even smoothstep — cannot produce a truly smooth yield pinch-off at saturation, what would be required within the additive framework, and how a multiplicative formulation resolves the problem cleanly.
Published

May 16, 2026

The polynomial offsets article compared three staking-offset shapes — linear, smoothstep, and quadratic — and noted that even the smoothstep offset, despite having zero slope at saturation, does not produce a yield curve that genuinely levels off as it approaches zero. This post explains exactly why, and explores two routes to a true smooth pinch-off: a modified offset within the additive framework, and a multiplicative reformulation that changes the base reward curve itself.

The residual slope problem

Under any additive staking-offset proposal, the net yield is

\[r_\text{net}(f) = K\bigl[r_{64}(f) - \phi(u)\,r_\text{sat}\bigr], \qquad u = f/f_\text{sat}\]

where \(r_{64}(f) = B_0 \cdot E / \sqrt{f \cdot S \cdot 10^9}\) is the gross yield at BASE_REWARD_FACTOR \(= 64\), \(K = B/64\) is the gross reward multiplier, \(\phi(u) \in [0,1]\) is the offset shape, and \(r_\text{sat} = r_{64}(f_\text{sat})\).

Differentiating with respect to \(f\) at \(f = f_\text{sat}\) (from below), using \(\mathrm{d}r_{64}/\mathrm{d}f = -r_\text{sat}/(2f_\text{sat})\):

\[\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}r_\text{net}}{\mathrm{d}f}\right|_{f_\text{sat}^-} = K\left[\underbrace{-\frac{r_\text{sat}}{2f_\text{sat}}}_{\text{from }1/\sqrt{f}\text{ decay}} - \underbrace{\frac{\phi'(1)}{f_\text{sat}}\,r_\text{sat}}_{\text{from offset shape}}\right]\]

The smoothstep offset (\(\phi = 3u^2 - 2u^3\)) has \(\phi'(1) = 0\), which zeroes out the second term. But the first term — the intrinsic \(1/\sqrt{f}\) decay of the gross yield — is always present. It cannot be cancelled by the offset’s own shape. The slope at saturation under smoothstep therefore remains

\[\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}r_\text{net}}{\mathrm{d}f}\right|_{f_\text{sat}^-} = -\frac{K_\text{smooth}\,r_\text{sat}}{2f_\text{sat}} \approx -\frac{2.53\,r_\text{sat}}{1.0}\]

This is non-zero regardless of how smooth the offset shape is at its endpoint. Setting \(\phi'(1) = 0\) is necessary for a soft landing, but not sufficient for a smooth one.

What the additive framework actually requires

For the net yield to arrive at zero with zero slope, the two contributions must cancel:

\[-\frac{r_\text{sat}}{2f_\text{sat}} - \frac{\phi'(1)}{f_\text{sat}}\,r_\text{sat} = 0 \implies \phi'(1) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\]

The offset shape must have a negative derivative at saturation — it must be decreasing as it approaches \(u = 1\). Since \(\phi(1) = 1\) (the offset equals the full base reward at saturation) and the function is decreasing at that point, it must have peaked above 1 somewhere before saturation and then come back down.

The modified cubic

The unique degree-3 polynomial satisfying \(\phi(0) = 0\), \(\phi'(0) = 0\), \(\phi(1) = 1\), \(\phi'(1) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\) is:

\[\phi_*(u) = \tfrac{7}{2}u^2 - \tfrac{5}{2}u^3\]

Its maximum occurs at \(u^* = 14/15 \approx 0.933\), where \(\phi_* \approx 1.016\) — about 1.6% above the full base reward. The offset first exceeds 1 at

\[u_0 = \frac{1 + \sqrt{11}}{5} \approx 0.863 \implies f_0 \approx 43.2\%\]

For staking ratios between 43.2% and 50%, the offset exceeds the gross reward, so the net yield is clipped to zero. The yield curve then approaches the zero-yield region from below with zero slope at exactly \(f_\text{sat} = 50\%\).

This gives a true smooth pinch-off, but at the cost of a roughly 7-percentage-point dead zone where net yield is already zero. Whether this is a feature (a strong near-saturation discouragement signal) or a flaw (inelegant; harder to motivate) is a design judgment.

A key mathematical constraint prevents doing better within the additive framework: any smooth function with \(\phi(1) = 1\) and \(\phi \leq 1\) everywhere on \([0,1]\) must have \(\phi'(1) \geq 0\). If \(\phi'(1) < 0\), the function would have to approach the value 1 from above, which requires it to exceed 1 somewhere. There is no monotone, bounded offset that achieves a smooth pinch-off.

Modifying the base reward curve

A cleaner route is to attack the problem at source: modify the gross yield curve \(r(f)\) itself so that it is flat at \(f_\text{sat}\), i.e. \(\tilde{r}'(f_\text{sat}) = 0\). Then, with any offset satisfying \(\phi'(1) = 0\) (such as smoothstep):

\[\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}r_\text{net}}{\mathrm{d}f}\right|_{f_\text{sat}^-} = K\bigl[\underbrace{\tilde{r}'(f_\text{sat})}_{=0} - \underbrace{\phi'(1)/f_\text{sat}\cdot r_\text{sat}}_{=0}\bigr] = 0\]

Both sources of slope vanish simultaneously.

Construction via blending

A natural way to flatten the gross yield at saturation: blend \(r(f)\) toward \(r_\text{sat}\) using a smooth interpolant \(\psi(u)\) with \(\psi(0) = 0\) and \(\psi(1) = 1\):

\[\tilde{r}(f) = \bigl(1 - \psi(u)\bigr)\,r_{64}(f) + \psi(u)\,r_\text{sat}\]

At low staking (\(u \approx 0\)), \(\psi \approx 0\) and \(\tilde{r} \approx r_{64}(f)\) — the current formula. At saturation (\(u = 1\)), \(\tilde{r} = r_\text{sat}\) exactly. The derivative at saturation is

\[\tilde{r}'(f_\text{sat}) = r_{64}'(f_\text{sat})\underbrace{(1-\psi(1))}_{=0} + \bigl(r_\text{sat} - r_{64}(f_\text{sat})\bigr)\frac{-\psi'(1)}{f_\text{sat}} = 0\]

The first term vanishes because \(\psi(1) = 1\). The second term vanishes because \(r_{64}(f_\text{sat}) = r_\text{sat}\) (the blend target equals the current formula at saturation). Crucially, this holds for any smooth \(\psi\) — it does not even require \(\psi'(1) = 0\). The flatness of \(\tilde{r}\) at saturation is a consequence of the blend endpoint coinciding with the current formula, not of any special choice of interpolant.

The multiplicative formulation

If the same function \(\psi\) is used for both the base reward blend and the offset shape (\(\phi = \psi\)), the formula simplifies dramatically:

\[r_\text{net}(f) = K\bigl[\tilde{r}(f) - \psi(u)\,r_\text{sat}\bigr] = K\bigl[\bigl(1-\psi(u)\bigr)r_{64}(f) + \psi(u)\,r_\text{sat} - \psi(u)\,r_\text{sat}\bigr] = K\,r_{64}(f)\,\bigl(1 - \psi(u)\bigr)\]

The modified base reward and the offset collapse into a single multiplicative factor:

\[\boxed{r_\text{net}(f) = \begin{cases} K\,r_{64}(f)\,\bigl(1 - \psi(f/f_\text{sat})\bigr) & f \le f_\text{sat} \\ 0 & f > f_\text{sat} \end{cases}}\]

where \(\psi\) is any smooth function with \(\psi(0) = 0\), \(\psi(1) = 1\), \(\psi'(1) = 0\). With smoothstep as \(\psi\):

\[r_\text{net}(f) = K\,r_{64}(f)\,\bigl(2u^3 - 3u^2 + 1\bigr) = K\,r_{64}(f)\,(1-u)^2(1+2u), \qquad u = f/f_\text{sat}\]

The factor \((1-u)^2(1+2u)\) goes from 1 at \(u=0\) to 0 at \(u=1\), with zero derivative at both endpoints. It is the “reverse smoothstep” — the complementary polynomial \(1 - (3u^2-2u^3)\).

Calibration

The multiplier \(K\) is fixed by requiring net yield to match current issuance at \(f_0 = 1/3\) (i.e. \(u_0 = 2/3\)):

\[K\,r_{64}(f_0)\,\bigl(1 - \psi(u_0)\bigr) = r_{64}(f_0) \implies K = \frac{1}{1 - \psi(2/3)}\]

With smoothstep: \(\psi(2/3) = 3(2/3)^2 - 2(2/3)^3 = 20/27\), so:

\[K = \frac{27}{7} \approx 3.857, \qquad B = 64K \approx 247\]

This is larger than the smoothstep additive-offset calibration (\(B \approx 162\)), because the multiplicative factor suppresses the gross yield throughout \([0, f_\text{sat}]\), requiring a larger upward rescaling to hit the calibration target at 33%.

Comparison with the additive-offset formulation

The multiplicative model is not equivalent to any additive-offset model with a clean polynomial offset shape. To see this, write the multiplicative model as an additive offset:

\[r_\text{net}(f) = K\bigl[r_{64}(f) - \underbrace{r_{64}(f)\,\psi(u)}_{\text{effective offset}}\bigr]\]

The effective offset is \(r_{64}(f) \cdot \psi(u)\), which scales with both the staking-dependent \(1/\sqrt{f}\) gross yield and the shape function \(\psi\). Unlike the standard additive offset \(\phi(u) \cdot r_\text{sat}\) (a fixed fraction of the yield at saturation), the multiplicative model’s implied offset shrinks with \(f\) — it is larger at low staking and smaller near saturation. This difference cannot be captured by any polynomial \(\phi(u)\) of finite degree.

The table below summarises the three main approaches to the smooth pinch-off problem:

Approach Yield slope at \(f_\text{sat}\) Dead zone? Spec complexity
Smoothstep offset \(-K_\text{sm}\,r_\text{sat}/(2f_\text{sat}) \neq 0\) None Additive offset only
Modified cubic offset (\(\phi_*\)) 0 ~43–50% Additive offset, non-standard shape
Multiplicative (smoothstep) 0 None Modified base reward formula

Spec implications

Changing the offset shape (additive model) is a relatively contained change — it modifies how attestation rewards are accounted for without touching the base reward formula itself. All penalties and other reward types remain anchored to the existing base reward.

Adopting the multiplicative model requires modifying the base reward formula at a more fundamental level. The base reward in the Ethereum spec is the common unit for:

  • Attestation rewards (source, target, head)
  • Sync committee rewards
  • Proposer rewards
  • Inactivity penalty rates
  • Slashing magnitudes

In the multiplicative model, all of these would scale with \((1-\psi(u))\) — their magnitude would shrink toward zero as staking approaches saturation. Whether this is desirable (penalties also weaken near saturation, reducing the cost of misbehaviour in an overstaked network) or problematic (penalties should arguably be strongest when the network is most at risk) is a separate governance question.

The additive offset sidesteps this entirely: the base reward, and therefore all penalties, are unchanged; only the net payout from attestation rewards is reduced by the offset deduction.

Summary

  • The \(1/\sqrt{f}\) gross yield has a non-zero slope at \(f_\text{sat}\), which “leaks through” into the net yield slope even when the offset shape has \(\phi'(1) = 0\). This is the fundamental reason smoothstep does not truly level off.
  • Within the additive framework, a smooth pinch-off requires \(\phi'(1) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\), which forces the offset to overshoot the full base reward before saturation, creating a dead zone from roughly 43% to 50% staked.
  • Modifying the base reward curve to be flat at saturation (via blending toward \(r_\text{sat}\)) eliminates the residual slope at source. Any offset with \(\phi'(1) = 0\) then gives a true smooth pinch-off.
  • The cleanest expression of this idea is the multiplicative model: \(r_\text{net}(f) = K \cdot r_{64}(f) \cdot (1-\psi(u))\), where the modified base reward and the offset collapse into a single factor. With \(\psi\) = smoothstep, this gives a yield curve that is exactly tangent to zero at 50% staked, with calibrated \(B \approx 247\).
  • The cost is a more invasive spec change: penalties and all other reward types scale with the same \((1-\psi(u))\) factor, which may or may not be desirable.
