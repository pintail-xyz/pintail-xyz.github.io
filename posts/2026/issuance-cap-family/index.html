A Power-Law Family of Issuance Caps for Ethereum

Comparing the properties of a one-parameter family of issuance modifications, of which the linear cap is one member.
Published

May 10, 2026

[Intro — reference to linear issuance cap post. The linear coefficient \(c(f) = k(1-2f)\) is one member of a natural one-parameter family. This article characterises that family and examines which value of the exponent \(\beta\) best satisfies the design objectives.]

tl;dr

  • The linear cap (\(\beta = 1\)) generalises naturally to a power-law family parameterised by \(\beta > 0\).
  • Every member shares the same two anchors: unchanged issuance at the current staking ratio \(f_0\) and zero issuance at the cap \(f = \tfrac{1}{2}\).
  • For \(\beta > 1\), the curve is strictly better on both stated objectives: stronger discouragement above \(f_0\) and stronger anti-discouragement protection below \(f_0\).
  • The cost of larger \(\beta\) is higher theoretical rewards at very low staking ratios.
  • The square root case (\(\beta = \tfrac{1}{2}\)) has an elegant geometric-mean interpretation but is weaker at capping the staking ratio than the linear form.

The Family

Definition

Define a one-parameter family of issuance multipliers:

\[c_\beta(f) = \begin{cases} \displaystyle\left(\frac{0.5 - f}{0.5 - f_0}\right)^{\!\beta} & f \leq \tfrac{1}{2} \\[6pt] 0 & f > \tfrac{1}{2} \end{cases}\]

Every member satisfies \(c_\beta(f_0) = 1\) (issuance unchanged at current staking level) and \(c_\beta(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 0\) (hard cap). The modified yield and issuance are:

\[r_\beta(f) = c_\beta(f)\,r(f), \qquad I_\beta(f) = c_\beta(f)\,I(f)\]

Key parameters

With \(f_0 = \tfrac{1}{3}\) (current staking ratio) so that \(0.5 - f_0 = \tfrac{1}{6}\):

\(\beta\) Form \(c_\beta(0)\) \(f^*\) Notes
\(\tfrac{1}{2}\) Square root \(\sqrt{3} \approx 1.73\) \(\tfrac{1}{4} = 25\%\) Geometric mean form; weaker cap
\(1\) Linear \(3\) \(\tfrac{1}{6} \approx 16.7\%\) Proposed in companion article
\(2\) Quadratic \(9\) \(\tfrac{1}{10} = 10\%\) Stronger cap; higher low-\(f\) rewards

where \(f^*\) is the staking ratio at which total issuance is maximised (anti-discouragement boundary), derived below.

Peak issuance

Total annual issuance under the modified curve is \(I_\beta(f) \propto (0.5 - f)^\beta \cdot \sqrt{f}\). Setting \(dI_\beta/df = 0\):

\[\frac{dI_\beta}{df} \propto \tfrac{1}{4} - f\!\left(\beta + \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; f^* = \frac{1}{4\beta + 2}\]

The anti-discouragement property (\(dI_\beta/df > 0\)) holds for all \(f < f^*\): total issuance grows as staking grows, so an adversary cannot profit by forcing validators to exit. For \(f > f^*\), total issuance falls as \(f\) increases, adding a natural market disincentive against excessive staking.

Dominance of \(\beta > 1\)

For \(f < f_0\): since \(\tfrac{0.5-f}{0.5-f_0} > 1\), raising the exponent increases \(c_\beta\). Higher \(\beta\) gives higher yield at low staking ratios — stronger anti-discouragement.

For \(f > f_0\): since \(\tfrac{0.5-f}{0.5-f_0} < 1\), raising the exponent reduces \(c_\beta\). Higher \(\beta\) gives lower yield above the current staking ratio — stronger downward pressure on the equilibrium.

The \(\beta > 1\) curves therefore dominate \(\beta = 1\) on both design objectives simultaneously. The trade-off is a larger \(c_\beta(0) = k^\beta\) — higher theoretical rewards at very low staking ratios — which is largely of academic interest given the current \(f_0 \approx \tfrac{1}{3}\).

Equilibrium Staking Ratio

The equilibrium staking ratio \(f_\text{eq}\) is set where the modified yield equals the marginal staker’s opportunity cost \(\rho\):

\[c_\beta(f_\text{eq})\,r(f_\text{eq}) = \rho\]

As \(\rho \to 0\) (liquid staking near-eliminates opportunity cost), \(f_\text{eq} \to \tfrac{1}{2}\) for any \(\beta > 0\): the hard cap binds. For finite \(\rho > 0\), higher \(\beta\) yields a strictly lower \(f_\text{eq}\), since \(c_\beta(f) < c_1(f)\) throughout the range \((f_0, \tfrac{1}{2})\).
