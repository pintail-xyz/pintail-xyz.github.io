Smooth Issuance Tapering: Polynomial Offsets for Ethereum

Comparing three polynomial staking-offset shapes — linear, smoothstep (3u²−2u³), and quadratic (2u−u²) — each calibrated to match current issuance at 33% staked. Smoothstep reaches zero at 50% with the gentlest slope of the three; the quadratic curve provides the strongest incentive for low-staking scenarios but at the cost of a much larger BASE_REWARD_FACTOR.
Published

May 15, 2026

The linear staking offset is a clean, implementable design: subtract a per-validator penalty proportional to the current staking ratio so that net issuance falls smoothly to zero at 50% staked. Its one notable shortcoming is that it approaches the zero-issuance point at a constant rate — the yield curve has a non-zero slope right up to the saturation point, making it a relatively sharp final approach.

This post explores two polynomial alternatives that share the same structural simplicity but reach zero more gently.

tl;dr

  • Three polynomial offset shapes — linear (\(u\)), smoothstep (\(3u^2-2u^3\)), and quadratic (\(2u-u^2\)) — all satisfy \(\phi(0)=0\) and \(\phi(1)=1\), so all three pin net issuance to zero at 50% staked.
  • Calibrating each curve so that net yield matches current issuance at \(f \approx 33\%\) determines the required BASE_REWARD_FACTOR (\(B\)): approximately 140, 162, and 233 for linear, smoothstep, and quadratic respectively.
  • Smoothstep and quadratic both have zero slope at \(u=1\), giving a softer final approach to zero; smoothstep also has zero slope at \(u=0\). In absolute terms, smoothstep reaches saturation at 38% of the linear slope, and quadratic at 55%.
  • Below 33% staked, the quadratic offset provides the highest yields (strongest stabiliser); above 33% it tapers more aggressively than smoothstep. Linear is the most conservative throughout.
  • The large calibrated \(B \approx 233\) for the quadratic option implies that all consensus-layer penalties scale up by a factor of \(\sim\!3.6\times\) relative to today — a significant governance consideration.

Setup

Let \(r(f) = B \cdot E / \sqrt{f \cdot S \cdot 10^9}\) be the gross nominal yield at staking ratio \(f\), computed with BASE_REWARD_FACTOR \(= B\) and epochs-per-year \(E\). Write \(r_\text{sat} = r(f_\text{sat})\) for the gross yield at the saturation point \(f_\text{sat} = 50\%\). The net yield under each proposal is:

\[r_\text{new}(f) = \begin{cases} K\,\bigl[r_{64}(f) - \phi(f/f_\text{sat})\cdot r_{64}(f_\text{sat})\bigr] & f \le f_\text{sat} \\ 0 & f > f_\text{sat} \end{cases}\]

where \(r_{64}\) denotes the yield with the current BASE_REWARD_FACTOR \(= 64\) and \(K = B/64\) is the gross reward multiplier. Above \(f_\text{sat}\) the offset equals the full base reward and net yield is zero.

The three offset shapes, defined on \(u = f/f_\text{sat} \in [0,1]\), are:

Variant \(\phi(u)\) \(\phi'(0)\) \(\phi'(1)\)
Linear \(u\) 1 1
Smoothstep \(3u^2 - 2u^3\) 0 0
Quadratic \(2u - u^2\) 2 0

All three satisfy \(\phi(0) = 0\) and \(\phi(1) = 1\), so all reach exactly zero net yield at 50% staked. Because each is a polynomial in \(u\), it extends naturally above \(u = 1\) without pathological behaviour (unlike sine-based shapes), so no clamping is needed — all three are strictly above 1 for \(u > 1\), keeping net yield at zero above saturation.

Calibration

To isolate the effect of shape from the effect of level, we calibrate \(K\) for each variant so that net yield at the current staking ratio \(f_0 = \tfrac{1}{3}\) equals today’s yield:

\[K \cdot \Bigl[r_{64}(f_0) - \phi\!\bigl(f_0/f_\text{sat}\bigr)\cdot r_{64}(f_\text{sat})\Bigr] = r_{64}(f_0)\]

Since \(r_{64}(f_\text{sat})/r_{64}(f_0) = \sqrt{f_0/f_\text{sat}} = \sqrt{2/3}\) and \(u_0 = f_0/f_\text{sat} = \tfrac{2}{3}\):

\[K = \frac{1}{1 - \phi(2/3)\cdot\sqrt{2/3}}\]

The three calibrated values are:

Variant \(\phi(2/3)\) \(\phi(2/3)\cdot\sqrt{2/3}\) \(K\) \(B = 64K\)
Linear \(2/3\) \(2\sqrt{6}/9 \approx 0.544\) \(9/(9-2\sqrt{6}) \approx 2.195\) \(\approx 140\)
Smoothstep \(20/27\) \(20\sqrt{6}/81 \approx 0.605\) \(81/(81-20\sqrt{6}) \approx 2.530\) \(\approx 162\)
Quadratic \(8/9\) \(8\sqrt{6}/27 \approx 0.726\) \(27/(27-8\sqrt{6}) \approx 3.647\) \(\approx 233\)

All three exact expressions involve \(\sqrt{6}\) because \(\sqrt{2/3} \cdot (2/3)^n\) simplifies to a rational multiple of \(\sqrt{6}\) for integer \(n\). None of the \(B\) values are round integers — see spec considerations below.

Properties

Yield

Below 33% — all three proposed curves lie above today’s yield, since \(K > 1\) in every case. The quadratic variant, with the largest \(K \approx 3.65\), provides the strongest stabilising incentive when staking is low: at 10% staking, it offers roughly five times today’s yield. The smoothstep and linear variants are progressively more conservative.

Above 33% — smoothstep and quadratic both taper faster than the linear variant. By 40% staking the linear variant still offers roughly 57% of today’s yield; smoothstep and quadratic are both closer to 40–45%. By 45%, linear is at about 28% of today’s yield while smoothstep and quadratic are both below 20%. The two smooth variants provide a stronger automatic market signal against excess staking throughout the danger zone.

Note that despite both having \(\phi'(1) = 0\), smoothstep and quadratic behave differently in the 33–50% range: smoothstep lies closer to the linear curve (it is sigmoidal and still rising steeply in the upper half), while the quadratic offset is larger throughout, causing more aggressive tapering above 33%.

Slope at saturation

The key qualitative difference among the variants is how abruptly net yield falls to zero at \(f = f_\text{sat}\). Computing \(dr_\text{new}/df\) from below at \(f = f_\text{sat}\), using \(dr/df|_{f_\text{sat}} = -r_\text{sat}/(2f_\text{sat})\):

\[\left.\frac{dr_\text{new}}{df}\right|_{f=f_\text{sat}^-} = -\frac{K\,r_\text{sat}}{f_\text{sat}}\left(\frac{1}{2} + \phi'(1)\right)\]

Since \(\phi'(1) = 1\) for linear and \(\phi'(1) = 0\) for both smooth curves, the slope at saturation is three times larger (in magnitude) for the linear variant than it would be if \(K\) were the same. But \(K\) differs across variants too, so the absolute slope ratios are:

Variant Slope factor \(K(\tfrac{1}{2}+\phi'(1))\) Relative to linear
Linear \(2.195 \times 1.5 = 3.29\) 1.00
Smoothstep \(2.530 \times 0.5 = 1.27\) 0.38
Quadratic \(3.647 \times 0.5 = 1.82\) 0.55

Smoothstep reaches zero with only 38% of the linear slope — the softest landing. Quadratic, despite its larger \(K\), still lands at 55% of the linear slope because \(\phi'(1) = 0\) halves the effective slope factor.

Total Issuance

The annual issuance is \(I(f) = r_\text{new}(f) \cdot f \cdot S\), which peaks at some \(f^* < f_\text{sat}\). Below \(f^*\), total issuance is increasing — higher staking draws more ETH into circulation. Above \(f^*\), additional staking actually reduces total issuance, providing an extra systemic pull back toward equilibrium.

The peak location \(f^*\) depends only on the offset shape \(\phi\), not on \(K\):

Variant \(f^*\)
Linear \(\approx 19.8\%\)
Smoothstep \(\approx 18.4\%\)
Quadratic \(\approx 14.8\%\)

All three lie well below the current \(\sim\!33\%\) staking ratio, so the anti-discouragement property holds in every case: at current staking levels, the equilibrium is already on the downward slope of the issuance curve, meaning the protocol is already automatically discouraging further staking growth.

Spec Considerations

All three offset shapes are simple polynomial expressions — one subtract and two multiplies for the smoothstep, one subtract and one multiply for the quadratic — and can be evaluated exactly in integer arithmetic with a small fixed-point representation. This is a practical advantage over transcendental functions (sine, cosine) which require approximation.

The calibrated BASE_REWARD_FACTOR values (≈ 140, 162, 233) are not round integers. In practice the spec would choose the nearest integer. Rounding from 161.9 to 162 for smoothstep, or from 233.4 to 233 for quadratic, introduces a deviation of less than 0.3% at the 33% calibration point — well within the precision of any practical implementation.

The original linear proposal used \(B = 128\) (\(K = 2\)) as a convenient round value, deliberately accepting a small yield reduction at deployment rather than requiring an exact match.

Penalty scaling. All consensus-layer penalties — inactivity leaks and slashing — scale linearly with BASE_REWARD_FACTOR. The calibrated \(B\) values imply penalty increases relative to today of:

Variant \(B\) Penalty multiplier
Current 64 \(1.0\times\)
Linear ≈ 140 \(\approx 2.2\times\)
Smoothstep ≈ 162 \(\approx 2.5\times\)
Quadratic ≈ 233 \(\approx 3.6\times\)

The quadratic option’s large \(K\) means validators face substantially higher inactivity and slashing penalties — a significant governance consideration that is independent of the yield question.

Discussion

Calibrated at 33%, the three polynomial shapes separate cleanly into two regime differences:

  1. Below 33%: how generously they reward stakers when participation is low. Quadratic is the most generous (largest K); linear the most conservative. All are above the current curve and all provide a stronger stabilising pull than today’s issuance schedule.

  2. Above 33%: how aggressively they taper against excess staking, and how smoothly they approach zero at 50%. Both smooth curves taper faster than linear; smoothstep is slightly gentler than quadratic in the 33–45% range. Both reach zero more gradually than linear.

The smoothstep polynomial occupies the middle ground in almost every respect: more aggressive than linear above 33% (providing a stronger discouraging signal), softer than linear at saturation (no abrupt cliff), and with a more moderate penalty multiplier than the quadratic. Its S-shaped curve — familiar from computer graphics and signal processing — has the additional intuitive property that the offset accelerates through the middle of the range and then decelerates as it approaches saturation.

The quadratic option is distinguished primarily by its behaviour at low staking: its concave shape means the offset rises quickly at first and then flattens, leaving a larger gross reward at low \(f\). This makes it the strongest automatic stabiliser if staking collapses but requires accepting substantially larger penalties as the cost of calibration.

The linear offset remains the most practically deployable: \(B = 128\) is a clean integer, it reduces to a single multiply-and-divide in the spec, and the penalty increase is the most modest. Whether the softer landing and stronger corrective properties of smoothstep justify the additional complexity (\(B \approx 162\), a non-integer) is a judgment call for the community.
