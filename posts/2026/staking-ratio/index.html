Ethereum’s Staking Ratio: The Tipping Point

An analysis of the growing staking ratio and what it means for Ethereum’s long-term economics.
Published

March 20, 2026

Over the last year, the environment for Ethereum staking has changed significantly. Regulatory changes in the US have emboldened providers of Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs) and Digital Asset Treasuries (DATs) to deploy their significant reserves to consume the apparently free lunch on offer from Ethereum’s proof-of-stake yield. As of March 2026, if we include Ether sitting in the staking entry queue, the proportion of the Ether supply which is staked (the staking ratio) has reached 1/3 for the first time. So now is a good time to ask: where might this lead? Is this desirable?

Questions about Ethereum proof-of-stake economics, and specifically the relationship between security and Ether issuance, are not new. As we will see, they have been debated since before the 2020 launch of the beacon chain. However the inherently contentious proposition of altering protocol incentives has led to such issues being sidelined in favour of uncontroversial upgrades or simply considered too hard to address. What I will do in this article is take a closer look at how we got here, where we appear to be headed, and the impact of this on Ethereum. Ultimately I will make the case that we can no longer afford to push this question away. Action to put us onto a more sustainable course should be a near-term priority.

Staking pioneers

Ethereum’s staking ecosystem has come a long way since the deposit contract for the proof-of-stake “beacon chain” was announced on 4 November 2020. This launch was the culmination of years of research and engineering, but it only delivered a parallel chain which came to consensus about nothing except the state of its own validator set.

Ethereum’s execution would continue to be secured by proof of work for an indeterminate period. Meanwhile the beacon chain was originally planned as “phase 0” of a rollout which would move through “phase 1” (data sharding) and “phase 2” (execution environments), and the idea of merging the beacon chain with the existing execution chain only came later. With so much still to spec, implement and test, there was very little discussion of when depositors might again have access to the Ether locked in the contract. They had no way of withdrawing their funds and were to take it on trust that it would work out at some unspecified point in the future.

Given how uncertain all of this seemed at the time, there was serious consideration given to the possibility that there might not be enough interest from ETH holders to reach the minimum threshold stake of 524,288 ETH. Indeed the concern that there might not be enough capital available to secure the chain had been long-running. Back when the beacon chain spec was still in flux in April 2019, the proposed issuance level was more than doubled, to target a 5.7% staking return at a targeted level of about 10M ETH staked, up from 2.5% return in the previous version of the spec. The result was that throughout the beacon chain’s gestation, the principal concern was whether there would be enough stake, and no consideration of whether there might be a level which was too much.

Liquid staking immediately changes the game

In all of these deliberations, the envisaged staking participant was a small-scale solo staker. That is, an individual enthusiast, locking up their own Ether and running their own node at home. It was acknowledged that bigger players would also be involved, and many were concerned that centralised providers might offer attractive delegated staking services which could lead to centralisation of the validator set. The hope was that by nurturing a vibrant enthusiast staker community, sufficient decentralisation would be achieved.

Regardless, within weeks of the beacon chain launch, Lido was accepting deposits. And by April 2021, prominent voices were arguing that:

Without staking derivatives, we might expect 15-30% of ETH to be staked. However, with staking derivatives, this number could be as high as 80-100%, because there is no additional cost to staking compared to non-staking.

— Hasu, Georgios Konstantopoulos (emphasis mine)

Of course, this didn’t happen overnight. There were lots of reasons to be cautious about staking in the early days. Solo stakers needed conviction and patience to lock up their Ether for an unknown duration (in the end, withdrawals finally became possible more than two years later). But all potential stakers had to accept the risks of new technology which had not been tested with real value at stake. Liquid staking solutions only compounded these risks, even if they did significantly alleviate the liquidity problem both by allowing staked positions to be used as collateral, and by offering a fast exit to depositors.

But as the market matured, the staking fraction steadily grew over the following year. The share going to liquid staking providers grew even faster. By May 2022, Danny Ryan sounded the alarm, highlighting the risks of LSD and calling for liquid staking protocols to self-limit to avoid breaching consensus thresholds, with many others including Vitalik chiming in in support. Weeks later, the Lido DAO voted almost unanimously that it would do no such thing. Nonetheless, Lido’s share of staked Ether peaked just below the 33% level around the same time.

This episode generated considerable disquiet within the Ethereum community, but it was short-lived. For one thing, Lido’s market share dropped somewhat, alleviating the concerns of some. But more importantly, the community was by this stage fully focused on the merge, generating a burst of euphoria upon its delivery in September 2022 along with some mainstream attention.

Staking Derisked

Throughout the whole period from the beacon chain launch, through the merge and afterwards, new stake continued to flow into the consensus layer at a fairly steady rate. Of course, staking flows could only be one-way at this stage, and with the advent of staking withdrawals in the Shapella upgrade in April 2023 traders worried about mass withdrawals impacting the Ether market.

A modest amount of stake did withdraw immediately after Shapella, almost entirely due to the exit of Kraken’s validators (the result of a settlement with the SEC which at the time viewed staking services as engaged in the sale of securities). But, by and large, the impact of enabling withdrawals was to derisk Ethereum staking, and the result was a massive inflow which quickly overwhelmed the Kraken withdrawals and led to a substantial entry queue of Ether waiting to join the active validator set.

Current trajectory

  • Staking rate is practically up only over the whole history of PoS on Ethereum
  • Dramatic rise in staking rate since SEC guidance has been masked by exogenous events (Kiln withdrawal, AAVE rate shock)
  • As staking rate rises further, the incentive to stake actually grows for lots of reasons
  • The declining APR has had no effect. The reason is that the net incentive to stake remains far greater than the costs associated with staking (and remain so all the way out to 100%)
  • Likely that in the long run Hasu/Georgios’s prediction will play out

Does this Matter?

  • What do we lose by going 100% staked?
  • We want to preserve raw, trust minimised ETH as the default asset. Any rewards from staking should match the costs.
  • Paying a subsidy in excess of this does not preserve a superior validator set. It just guarantees eventual total capture by large entities (solo stakers can’t go on)

Scratch notes

Aims of article are to:

  1. Remind people that the issuance problem hasn’t been solved
  2. Draw attention to the problems of a highly-staked scenario
  3. Use the framing of market-set yield to explain why this problem is set to continue getting worse

To do this effectively I must:

  1. Tell a story that an ordinary reader finds compelling and can understand without much effort.
  2. Use simple language as far as possible and not get bogged down in details which could throw readers off.
  3. Grab the reader’s attention! Someone who cares about this stuff shouldn’t be able to tear their eyes away!
  4. Be forthright in confronting the positions of people/groups with an agenda or self-interested reason to oppose reform.
  5. But not unnecessarily antagonise people that I’d like to win over.
  6. Be thoughtful and deliberate with the terms I use for things
