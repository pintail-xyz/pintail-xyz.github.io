Smooth Issuance Tapering: Sine and S-Curve Offsets for Ethereum

Two alternatives to a linear staking offset — a sine-shaped and an S-curve (sin²) offset — calibrated to match current issuance at 33% staked, with gentler behaviour near the saturation point and stronger tapering in between.
Published

May 15, 2026

[Intro — reference to the linear tapering post; motivation for exploring alternative offset shapes; note that this post focuses purely on shape differences by anchoring all three curves to the same point at today’s staking ratio.]

tl;dr

  • The linear staking offset is a clean, implementable design, but its offset grows at a constant rate and its net yield has a non-zero slope right up to the saturation point — a sharp final approach to zero.
  • Two alternative offset shapes — sine (\(\sin\frac{\pi f}{2f_\text{sat}}\)) and S-curve (\(\sin^2\frac{\pi f}{2f_\text{sat}}\)) — each have zero slope at \(f_\text{sat}\), giving a softer final approach, but require a larger gross reward factor (BASE_REWARD_FACTOR) to compensate. Both shapes are applied only over \([0, f_\text{sat}]\); above 50% the offset equals the full base reward and net yield is zero. (A naive extension of the sine shape beyond \(f_\text{sat}\) would cause its offset to decrease and net yield to re-emerge around \(f \approx 67\%\); the clamping prevents this.)
  • Calibrating all three curves to match current issuance at \(f \approx 33\%\) reveals the shape differences cleanly: below 33% all three offer higher yields than today; above 33% sine and S-curve are more aggressive than linear; and all three reach zero at 50%.
  • Near saturation, sine has roughly half the slope of linear, and S-curve roughly 40% — both produce a materially softer landing.
  • The calibrated BASE_REWARD_FACTOR values are approximately 140, 165, and 219 for the linear, S-curve, and sine proposals respectively — none of them round numbers.
  • The sine curve’s calibration has an exact closed form: \(K_\text{sine} = 2 + \sqrt{2}\), because \(\sin(\pi/3)\cdot\sqrt{2/3} = 1/\sqrt{2}\) exactly.

Setup

All three proposals share the same structural form. Let \(r(f) = B \cdot E / \sqrt{f \cdot S \cdot 10^9}\) denote the gross nominal yield computed with BASE_REWARD_FACTOR \(= B\), let \(r_\text{sat} = r(f_\text{sat})\), and let \(\phi(u) \in [0,1]\) be the offset shape function for \(u = f/f_\text{sat} \in [0,1]\). Net yield under each proposal is:

\[r_\text{new}(f) = \begin{cases} K\,\bigl[r_\text{64}(f) - \phi(f/f_\text{sat})\cdot r_\text{64}(f_\text{sat})\bigr] & f \le f_\text{sat} \\ 0 & f > f_\text{sat} \end{cases}\]

where \(r_\text{64}\) denotes the yield computed with the current BASE_REWARD_FACTOR \(= 64\), and \(K = B/64\) is the gross reward multiplier. Above \(f_\text{sat}\) the offset equals the full gross base reward and net yield is zero.

The three offset shapes, defined on \([0, f_\text{sat}]\), are:

Variant \(\phi(u)\), \(u = f/f_\text{sat} \in [0,1]\)
Linear \(u\)
Sine \(\sin(\pi u/2)\)
S-curve \(\sin^2(\pi u/2) = \tfrac{1-\cos(\pi u)}{2}\)

All three satisfy \(\phi(0) = 0\) and \(\phi(1) = 1\), so net issuance reaches zero at \(f = f_\text{sat} = 50\%\).

Why the explicit \(f > f_\text{sat}\) floor is needed for sine and S-curve. The linear offset \(\phi(u) = u\) grows without bound past \(u = 1\), so it always exceeds the base reward above \(f_\text{sat}\) and the \(\max(0,\ldots)\) floor suffices. The sine and S-curve shapes behave differently: \(\sin(\pi u/2)\) peaks at \(u=1\) and then decreases (entering the descending half of the cycle), falling to zero at \(u=2\). Without the explicit floor, the offset would eventually drop back below the base reward — around \(f \approx 67\%\) for the sine curve — causing net yield to turn positive again above saturation. Applying the shape only on \([0, f_\text{sat}]\) and holding the offset at the full base reward above that point eliminates this pathology.

Calibration

To isolate the effect of shape from the effect of level, we calibrate \(K\) for each variant so that the net yield at the current staking ratio \(f_0 = \tfrac{1}{3}\) equals today’s yield:

\[K \cdot \Bigl[r_\text{64}(f_0) - \phi\!\bigl(f_0/f_\text{sat}\bigr)\cdot r_\text{64}(f_\text{sat})\Bigr] = r_\text{64}(f_0)\]

Since \(r_\text{64}(f_\text{sat})/r_\text{64}(f_0) = \sqrt{f_0/f_\text{sat}} = \sqrt{2/3}\) and \(f_0/f_\text{sat} = \tfrac{2}{3}\):

\[K = \frac{1}{1 - \phi(2/3)\cdot\sqrt{2/3}}\]

The three calibrated values are:

Variant \(\phi(2/3)\) \(\phi(2/3)\cdot\sqrt{2/3}\) \(K\) \(B = 64K\)
Linear \(2/3\) \(2\sqrt{6}/9 \approx 0.544\) \(\approx 2.195\) \(\approx 140\)
S-curve \(3/4\) \(\sqrt{6}/4 \approx 0.612\) \(4/(4-\sqrt{6}) \approx 2.580\) \(\approx 165\)
Sine \(\sqrt{3}/2\) \(1/\sqrt{2}\) \(2 + \sqrt{2} \approx 3.414\) \(\approx 219\)

The sine calibration is exact: \(\sin(\pi/3)\cdot\sqrt{2/3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\), which gives \(K_\text{sine} = 1/(1-1/\sqrt{2}) = 2+\sqrt{2}\).

None of the three values of \(B\) are round integers, which is worth noting for spec purposes — see the discussion below.

Properties

Yield

The curves separate into two distinct regimes once calibrated at 33%:

Below 33% — all three proposed curves lie above today’s yield. The sine curve is most generous at low staking (because its large K = 2+√2 amplifies the gross reward), providing roughly five times today’s yield at 10% staking. The S-curve and linear variants are intermediate. This is a natural stabiliser: if staking falls, the yield rise is larger under each proposed curve than under the current one, attracting validators back more strongly.

Above 33% — the sine and S-curve taper faster than the linear variant. At 40% staking, the linear variant offers about 57% of today’s yield, while the sine and S-curve are closer to 46–47%. By 45%, the linear variant is at 28% of today’s yield versus about 17–19% for the others. The sine and S-curve therefore provide a stronger market signal against excess staking throughout the danger zone.

Slope at saturation

The most important qualitative difference between the variants is the slope of the net yield as \(f \to f_\text{sat}\). Since \(\phi'(1) = 0\) for both the sine and S-curve (the offset is flat at saturation), the slope of the net yield is:

\[\left.\frac{dr_\text{new}}{df}\right|_{f=f_\text{sat}} = \begin{cases} -3K\,r_\text{sat}/f_\text{sat} & \text{linear}\\ -K\,r_\text{sat}/f_\text{sat} & \text{sine or S-curve} \end{cases}\]

With the calibrated K values:

Variant Slope magnitude at \(f_\text{sat}\) Relative to linear
Linear \(\propto 3 \times 2.195 = 6.58\) 1.00
Sine \(\propto 1 \times 3.414 = 3.41\) 0.52
S-curve \(\propto 1 \times 2.580 = 2.58\) 0.39

The sine curve approaches zero with about half the slope of the linear version. The S-curve approaches with about 40% of the linear slope — the softest landing of the three. Validators at 48–49% staking face a more gradual final reduction; there is no abrupt cliff.

Total Issuance

The calibration anchors all three curves to the current issuance at 33%. Below 33%, the sine and S-curve rise higher than the linear variant because their larger \(K\) amplifies the gross reward more; above 33%, they taper more steeply to zero.

Anti-Discouragement

Total issuance is increasing in \(f\) for \(f < f^*\) and decreasing thereafter. The peak \(f^*\) depends only on the shape \(\phi\), not on \(K\), and is the same under calibrated or uncalibrated variants:

Variant \(f^*\)
Linear \(\approx 19.8\%\)
S-curve \(\approx 18\%\)
Sine \(\approx 15.5\%\)

All three lie well below the current \(\sim\!33\%\) staking ratio, so the anti-discouragement property is preserved in all cases.

Spec Considerations

The sine and S-curve shapes cannot be computed exactly in integer arithmetic. A rational approximation or lookup table would be required in the consensus-layer spec.

The calibrated BASE_REWARD_FACTOR values (\(\approx 140\), \(165\), \(219\)) are not round numbers. In practice, the spec would round to the nearest integer, introducing a small (\(< 0.5\%\)) discrepancy at the 33% calibration point.

For comparison: the original linear proposal used BASE_REWARD_FACTOR \(= 128\) (\(K = 2\)) as a convenient round value, accepting an \(\sim\!9\%\) yield reduction at deployment rather than an exact match. An analogous choice for the sine proposal would be, say, \(B = 218\), accepting a similarly small deviation at 33%.

The penalty for non-participation scales with \(B\), so the larger values required by sine and S-curve also increase slashing and inactivity penalties — by a factor of \(3.4\times\) for sine vs. \(2.2\times\) for linear relative to today. This is a governance consideration independent of the yield question.

Discussion

[Summary of tradeoffs; which variant to recommend; comparison with other proposals; path to an EIP.]

With the yield level anchored at 33%, the three curves differ in only two ways:

  1. Below 33%: how generously they reward stakers when participation is low. Sine offers the most headroom; linear the least. All are above the current curve.

  2. Above 33%: how aggressively they push back against excess staking, and how smoothly they approach zero. Sine and S-curve are steeper in the 33–45% range; all three are gentler at the very end, with S-curve gentlest.

If the goal is a strong automatic stabiliser — a curve that reacts sharply to staking above 33% while providing a soft landing at saturation — the S-curve has the better-motivated shape. Its sin² form is the raised cosine from signal processing: zero slope at both ends by design, maximum responsiveness in the middle. It is more aggressive than linear in the danger zone (above 25%) and gentler near saturation.

The linear offset remains the most practically deployable: its BASE_REWARD_FACTOR of 128 is a clean integer, and it reduces to a single multiply-and-divide in the spec. Whether the soft landing and stronger corrective properties of sine or S-curve justify the added implementation complexity and larger gross reward factor is a question for the community.
